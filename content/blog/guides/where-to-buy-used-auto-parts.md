## Local Junkyards and Self-Service Yards {#local-junkyards}

Self-service junkyards, also called pull-a-part lots or U-Pull-It yards, remain the cheapest place to buy used auto parts in the country. You pay a small entry fee (typically $2 to $5), walk the rows, find your vehicle match, and pull the part yourself. Pricing runs 40% to 70% below what a full-service yard charges for the same part.

### How it works

You show up with your own tools. The yard has rows of vehicles organized roughly by make or section. You find a matching vehicle, unbolt the part, bring it to the counter, and pay the posted price. Most self-service yards use flat-rate pricing boards: a door is one price, an alternator is another, regardless of make or model.

### Pricing

Self-service yards are hard to beat on price. An alternator might run $25 to $40. A door assembly, $50 to $100. Engines and transmissions are priced higher but still well below full-service salvage pricing. For a breakdown of typical pricing across part categories, see our [used auto parts pricing guide](/blog/market/used-auto-parts-pricing-guide).

### Speed and reliability

Speed depends entirely on your own hustle. If the part is in the yard, you walk out with it in an hour. If it's not, you've burned a trip. There's no way to check inventory remotely at most self-service lots. Reliability is as-is: you inspect it yourself, and there's no guarantee it works until you install it.

### Return policy

Most self-service yards sell everything as-is, no returns. A few larger chains like LKQ Pick Your Part offer limited exchange windows (typically 30 days with receipt), but that's the exception.

**Best for:** Budget-conscious DIY owners, shops needing common body panels, and anyone who doesn't mind wrenching in a yard for an hour.

## Car-Part.com {#car-part-com}

Car-Part.com is the largest online salvage parts database in North America, indexing over 200 million parts from thousands of recyclers ([Car-Part.com](https://www.car-part.com), 2025). If a part exists in a participating yard's inventory system, it shows up here. For most people searching "used auto parts online," this is where they start.

### How it works

Enter the year, make, model, and part name. The system returns matching listings from yards that have uploaded that part to their inventory management software. Each listing shows the yard name, location, price, mileage (when available), and sometimes a photo. You then call or email the yard to confirm availability and arrange purchase or shipping.

### Pricing

Prices are set by each individual yard. You'll see a range of quotes for the same part from different sellers. This is useful for comparison shopping, but it also means you need to factor in shipping costs when buying from yards outside your area.

### Speed and reliability

Search results appear instantly. The lag is in confirmation: a part listed on Car-Part.com may have been sold since the last inventory sync. Some yards update daily; others sync weekly or less. You won't know the part is actually available until you contact the yard. For hard-to-find parts, this step can eat time if your first three results are already sold.

### Return policy

Return policies are set by each yard individually. Most full-service recyclers offer 30- to 90-day warranties on major mechanical parts (engines, transmissions) and shorter windows or no returns on body and electrical parts. Always ask before buying.

**Best for:** Shops and individuals searching for specific parts across a wide geographic area. The broadest net you can cast online.

## eBay Motors {#ebay-motors}

eBay Motors carries a massive volume of used auto parts from salvage yards, individual sellers, and parts resellers. It works well for both common and obscure parts because the seller base includes professional recyclers alongside individual car owners parting out vehicles.

### How it works

Search by year, make, model, and part name, or by OEM part number for precision. Filter by condition (used, refurbished, new aftermarket), price range, and seller location. Most listings include photos, condition notes, and compatibility charts. You buy online and the part ships to you.

### Pricing

eBay pricing is competitive because sellers undercut each other. You'll often find parts priced 20% to 40% below a local yard's asking price, especially for common components. Shipping adds cost, though: a $60 fender can carry $40 in freight. Watch the total landed price, not just the part price.

### Speed and reliability

Shipping times vary from 2 to 10 business days depending on the seller and part size. Reliability depends on the seller's reputation. Check the feedback score and read recent reviews. Established sellers with thousands of transactions are generally safe. New sellers with thin histories carry more risk.

### Return policy

eBay's Money Back Guarantee covers most transactions. If the part doesn't match the listing description or arrives damaged, you can return it. Some sellers also accept returns for fitment issues. This buyer protection is a real advantage over buying from a yard with no return policy.

**Best for:** Individual car owners who want buyer protection, comparison shopping, and shipping to their door. Also useful for shops sourcing rare or discontinued parts.

## Facebook Marketplace {#facebook-marketplace}

Facebook Marketplace and Facebook groups dedicated to auto parts have become a significant channel for buying used parts. The platform works best for local transactions: you search your area, find a seller, and meet up or arrange local delivery.

### How it works

Search "used auto parts" plus your make and model, or join regional auto parts groups. Sellers post photos and prices. You message them, negotiate, and arrange pickup or meet. Some groups specialize in specific makes (Ford trucks, Honda Civic parts, etc.) and have active communities.

### Pricing

Prices are all over the map. You'll find deals well below yard pricing from individuals parting out a vehicle who just want the car off their property. You'll also find delusional pricing from people who think their rusted fender is worth retail. Negotiation is expected.

### Speed and reliability

Speed is fast for local deals: you can have the part the same day. Reliability is the weak spot. There's no standardized condition grading, no buyer protection, and no recourse if the part is bad. You're trusting photos and the seller's word.

### Return policy

None. This is a private sale. Once you pay and take the part, it's yours. Some sellers will work with you if there's an obvious problem, but there's no formal process.

**Best for:** Local bargain hunters, people parting out specific vehicles, and anyone who doesn't mind spending time scrolling and messaging. Poor choice for shops that need consistent supply.

## OEM vs Aftermarket vs Salvage: Which Should You Buy? {#oem-vs-aftermarket-vs-salvage}

Three categories of replacement parts exist, and choosing the right one depends on the component, your budget, and how long you need it to last.

### OEM (Original Equipment Manufacturer)

OEM parts are made by the same manufacturer that supplied the part to the automaker. A new OEM headlight for a 2020 Camry is the same part Toyota installed at the factory. New OEM is the most expensive option, often 2x to 4x the aftermarket price.

**Used OEM** parts from salvage yards offer the same fit and quality at 50% to 75% below new OEM pricing. This is the sweet spot for major components like engines, transmissions, doors, fenders, and headlights. The part was designed for your exact vehicle. It just has miles on it.

### Aftermarket

Aftermarket parts are manufactured by third-party companies to fit the same application. Quality ranges widely, from budget brands with loose tolerances to premium brands that match or exceed OEM specs. Pricing sits between used OEM and new OEM.

Aftermarket works well for wear items: brake pads, rotors, filters, belts, hoses, spark plugs. For body panels, fit-and-finish can vary. A cheap aftermarket fender might need extra adjustment to align properly. For mechanical internals (engine gaskets, suspension bushings), reputable aftermarket brands like Moog, Dorman, and Denso deliver solid quality.

### Salvage (used OEM from a recycler)

Salvage parts are used OEM components pulled from end-of-life vehicles by licensed auto recyclers. The part is genuine OEM; it just came off a wrecked or retired car. Salvage pricing typically runs 50% to 75% below new OEM.

The risk with salvage is unknown history. A transmission from a car with 120,000 miles has 120,000 miles of wear. Reputable recyclers test major mechanical parts and offer warranties. Less reputable ones sell as-is. Always ask about mileage, testing, and warranty before buying mechanical salvage parts.

For the parts in highest demand from salvage yards, see [which parts dismantlers request most](/blog/market/most-requested-used-auto-parts).

**The rule of thumb:** Use salvage OEM for major components (engines, transmissions, body panels, glass, lights). Use aftermarket for wear items and maintenance parts. Use new OEM only when no suitable alternative exists, or when the repair is on a newer vehicle still under factory warranty.

## How to Verify Fitment and Avoid Bad Buys {#fitment-and-buying-tips}

Buying the wrong part wastes money and time. These steps reduce that risk significantly.

### Match by OEM part number

Every major component has a part number stamped, etched, or printed on a label. Before you search anywhere, find the OEM part number for the part you need. Check the existing part on your vehicle, look it up in an OEM parts catalog (most manufacturer websites offer free lookup by VIN), or use a resource like RealOEM for BMW or Toyota's parts catalog.

When you find a listing, ask the seller for the part number on the unit they're selling. If the numbers match, fitment is confirmed. This is the single most reliable way to avoid buying the wrong part.

### Use interchange databases

Not every matching part shares the same part number. Automakers often use the same component across multiple model years or across different models. Interchange databases like Hollander (used by most professional recyclers) and Car-Part.com's interchange lookup tell you which parts from other vehicles will fit yours.

For example, a 2018 Camry headlight might interchange with 2019 and 2020 model years. The interchange database confirms this compatibility. Without it, you're guessing.

### Verify year, make, model, and trim

Trim level matters more than most buyers realize. A base-model Accord and an EX-L Accord may use different headlights, different bumper covers, different engines. Always specify the full trim level and engine size when requesting or searching for parts.

### Inspect photos carefully

For online purchases, request multiple photos from different angles. Look for cracks, chips, corrosion, and mounting tab damage. On mechanical parts, look for oil leaks, corrosion on electrical connectors, and signs of previous repair. If the seller won't send detailed photos, move on.

### Ask the right questions

Before buying any used mechanical part, ask:

- What is the mileage on the part (or the donor vehicle)?
- Was the part tested? How?
- Is there a warranty? How long? What does it cover?
- What is the return policy if the part doesn't work?
- Is there a core charge?

These questions separate professional recyclers from fly-by-night sellers. A legitimate yard will answer all of them without hesitation.

## Warranty and Return Considerations {#warranty-and-returns}

Warranty coverage varies dramatically across buying channels. Knowing what you're getting before you pay saves headaches after.

**Full-service salvage yards** typically offer 30- to 90-day warranties on engines, transmissions, and other major mechanical parts. Some national networks offer extended warranties (up to 6 months or 1 year) for an additional fee. Body parts and electrical components often carry shorter warranties or none at all.

**Self-service yards** sell as-is. You pulled it, you own it. A few chains like LKQ Pick Your Part offer 30-day exchange policies, but most independent pull-a-part lots do not.

**eBay Motors** provides the eBay Money Back Guarantee, which covers parts that don't match the listing description. This is effectively a return policy backed by the platform, not the seller. It's one of the strongest buyer protections available for used parts.

**Amazon** applies its standard 30-day return window to most auto parts, including used and renewed items. The A-to-Z Guarantee adds another layer if the seller doesn't cooperate.

**Facebook Marketplace** offers no buyer protection for local pickup transactions. Once you hand over cash and take the part, your only recourse is the seller's goodwill.

**Car-Part.com** is a search platform, not a seller. Warranty terms depend entirely on the yard you buy from. Always confirm before placing an order.

**RockAuto** accepts returns within 30 days for most new parts. Return shipping is your cost.

If warranty matters to you (and for engines and transmissions it should) buy from a full-service yard with a written warranty, or through eBay or Amazon where platform-level buyer protection exists.
