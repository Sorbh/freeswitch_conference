## Where Should You Source Salvage Vehicles? {#where-to-source}

The two largest US salvage auction platforms, Copart and IAA (now part of RB Global), move millions of vehicles annually. Copart alone processed roughly 4 million vehicles in fiscal year 2024 ([Copart 10-K Filing](https://www.copart.com/Content/US/Investors), 2024). But auctions aren't the only sourcing channel, and they're not always the best one for every vehicle type.

### Online salvage auctions

Copart and IAA are where most dismantlers start. Both platforms list vehicles with photos, damage type, odometer readings, and run-and-drive status. You can bid from your desk, which saves a full day at the lot. Registration requires a state dealer or dismantler license in most jurisdictions.

The convenience comes with competition. Popular late-model vehicles attract dozens of bidders, and buyer's premiums add 10-18% on top of your winning bid. That overhead matters when you're calculating per-vehicle profit.

### Insurance company direct deals

Some carriers sell totaled vehicles directly to licensed dismantlers, skipping the auction entirely. These deals carry lower prices because the insurer avoids auction listing fees. Building relationships with local adjusters and claims managers takes time. But once you're on their vendor list, you'll see vehicles before they reach the public platforms.

### Tow yard relationships

Tow operators handle abandoned and unclaimed vehicles constantly. Many don't want the hassle of listing them at auction. A standing offer to buy abandoned vehicles at a flat rate creates a steady, low-cost supply line. The inventory skews older and less predictable, but the price often makes up for the risk.

### Public auctions and estate sales

Municipal impound lots and estate liquidations occasionally surface vehicles most dismantlers overlook. Competition is lower than online platforms. Inventory quality varies, so treat these as a supplement to your primary channels, not a replacement.

> **Citation Capsule**: Copart processed approximately 4 million vehicles in fiscal year 2024, according to its SEC 10-K filing. Online salvage auctions represent the largest sourcing channel for US dismantlers, but direct insurance deals and tow yard relationships can deliver lower per-vehicle costs through reduced competition and zero auction fees.

## The Profit Math: A Worked Dismantling Example {#profit-math}

A salvage buy only makes sense when the parts revenue outweighs total acquisition and labor costs. Net margins for salvage yards range from 10% to 20% of revenue ([FinModelsLab](https://finmodelslab.com/blogs/how-much-makes/how-much-business-owner-makes-junkyard), 2025). Better sourcing decisions push you toward the high end.

Here's a real example. You buy a 2019 Honda CR-V with front-end collision damage at Copart for $1,200.

**Parts you can pull:**

| Part | Estimated Revenue |
|---|---|
| Transmission | $650 |
| Motor | $800 |
| Headlight assemblies (pair) | $180 |
| Catalytic converter | $250 |
| Misc (mirrors, doors, electronics) | $400 |
| **Total parts revenue** | **$2,280** |

Subtract your purchase price ($1,200) and dismantling labor, roughly 4-6 hours at $350 all-in. That's $730 net profit from one vehicle.

That's the clean version. In practice, you also pay buyer's premium (18% on Copart, or $216), and transport runs $150-$500 depending on distance. On a $1,200 buy with $300 in transport and premium, your realistic net drops to $380-$500. Still solid. But it shows why the bid ceiling matters so much. Every dollar over your target eats directly into profit.

The key question before every bid: can you sell the parts fast enough to justify tying up the capital? A vehicle that sits six months waiting for buyers costs you in space, handling, and depreciation. Check demand first.

## How Do You Evaluate a Vehicle Before Bidding? {#evaluation-checklist}

Vehicles in the 3-7 year age range consistently yield the best dismantling returns. The average age of vehicles on US roads hit 12.6 years in 2024 ([S&P Global Mobility](https://www.spglobal.com/mobility/en/index.html), 2024), which means parts for 3-7 year old models carry strong demand from repair shops and retail buyers.

### Damage pattern assessment

Not all damage is equal. Front-end collisions are often the best buys for dismantlers because the drivetrain, rear body, and interior typically survive intact. Rear-end damage can preserve the engine bay but destroy the trunk, tailgate, and sometimes the fuel system. Side impacts and rollovers tend to damage the most components across the vehicle, making yields unpredictable.

Over the years, we've found that front-end hits on late-model SUVs are the sweet spot. The bumper, grille, and hood are gone, but everything behind the firewall usually checks out. You lose maybe $300 in crushed body parts but keep $1,500 or more in drivetrain and electrical.

### The mileage and year sweet spot

Vehicles under 80,000 miles with 3-7 years of age hit the demand curve perfectly. Parts from these vehicles are new enough that repair shops trust them and old enough that OEM replacements are expensive. Below 3 years, salvage prices run too high because clean-title buyers bid them up. Above 7 years, parts demand softens and prices drop.

### Parts demand check

Before you bid on anything, check whether the parts you'll pull actually have buyers. Search [Car-Part.com](https://www.car-part.com/), which indexes over 200 million parts from yards nationwide ([Car-Part.com](https://www.car-part.com/), 2026). If twenty yards already list the same transmission, supply is high and your sale price drops. If only two or three list it, you've found a gap.

### Bid ceiling discipline

Set your maximum bid before the auction starts. Write it down. Every dollar above that number comes directly out of your profit. The math doesn't care how much you wanted the vehicle.

Calculate your ceiling like this: estimated parts revenue minus labor minus transport minus fees minus your target profit. What's left is the most you can pay. If bidding pushes past that number, let it go. Another vehicle will come up tomorrow.

## Copart vs IAA: Which Platform Fits Dismantlers Better? {#copart-vs-iaa}

Copart and IAA (RB Global) together control the majority of US salvage vehicle auctions. Copart operates over 200 locations across North America ([Copart.com](https://www.copart.com/), 2026). IAA runs a comparable network. Both serve dismantlers, but their fee structures and inventory mixes differ in ways that affect your bottom line.

### Fee structures

Copart charges a buyer's premium that scales with the sale price. For basic members, expect roughly 18% on top of your winning bid. IAA's fee structure varies by membership tier, but licensed dealers and dismantlers often pay lower premiums in the 10-15% range. Both platforms charge additional gate fees, environmental fees, and title processing fees that add $50-$100 per vehicle.

Don't ignore these when calculating your bid ceiling. On a $1,500 vehicle, the difference between 18% and 12% buyer's premium is $90. Across 50 vehicles per year, that's $4,500 in savings.

### Inventory differences

Copart tends to carry more insurance-consigned vehicles, including newer models and higher-value total losses. IAA's mix skews slightly toward older fleet vehicles and dealer trade-ins in some regions. Both platforms carry the full range, but checking both before each buy expands your options.

### Bidding strategies that work

Don't bid early. On both platforms, early bids signal interest and attract competition. Place your bid in the final moments of the auction window. Set your ceiling, wait, and bid once. If you don't win, move on. There's no shortage of vehicles.

Use proxy bidding to set your maximum and let the system bid incrementally on your behalf. This prevents the emotional ratcheting that happens when you're watching a countdown timer.

Here's something most guides won't tell you: buy on rainy days and holiday weeks. Auction participation drops noticeably during bad weather and around holidays. The same vehicle that draws 15 bidders on a Tuesday in June might attract 6 on a Wednesday before Thanksgiving. Fewer bidders means lower prices.


## What Mistakes Cost Salvage Buyers the Most Money? {#costly-mistakes}

The Automotive Recyclers Association estimates roughly 9,000 auto recycling businesses operate across the US ([ARA](https://a-r-a.org/), 2025). The ones that struggle with sourcing tend to make the same five mistakes repeatedly.

### Bidding on emotion

You've been watching a 2021 Civic for three days. You've already imagined the parts on your shelves. The auction starts and someone outbids you by $50. So you bid again. And again. This is how a $1,200 buy becomes a $1,800 buy and your profit evaporates. The vehicle doesn't care how much time you spent researching it. Stick to your ceiling.

### Ignoring transport costs

A vehicle listed 200 miles from your yard might look like a great deal until you add $400 in transport. Copart offers in-house shipping, but rates vary by distance and vehicle size ([Copart.com](https://www.copart.com/), 2026). Always factor transport into your bid ceiling, not after you've won.

### Skipping the demand check

Buying a vehicle because the price is low without checking whether anyone wants its parts is how yards end up with rows of unsaleable inventory. Check Car-Part.com and eBay sold listings before every bid. If supply already outweighs demand for the model's key parts, pass.

### Overpaying for clean-title vehicles

Some dismantlers chase clean-title vehicles thinking they'll yield better parts. Sometimes that's true. But clean-title vehicles attract retail buyers who will always outbid you because they see a drivable car, not a parts donor. Focus on salvage-title and insurance total-loss vehicles where you're competing against other dismantlers, not retail shoppers.

### Buying without a plan for the hull

After you pull the profitable parts, you've still got 2,500 pounds of steel. If you don't have a scrap buyer or a hull disposal arrangement, that weight sits on your lot eating space. Line up your scrap relationship before you start buying at volume.

> **Citation Capsule**: Approximately 9,000 auto recycling businesses operate across the United States, according to the Automotive Recyclers Association. The most common sourcing mistake among these operations is emotional bidding, pushing past calculated bid ceilings chasing vehicles that looked good on screen but don't pencil out on paper.

## How Do Demand Signals Improve Your Sourcing? {#demand-signals}

The smartest sourcing strategy isn't just finding cheap vehicles. It's buying vehicles that contain the parts people are actively requesting. Car-Part.com's index of over 200 million parts gives you supply data ([Car-Part.com](https://www.car-part.com/), 2026). But knowing what buyers want right now requires a different signal.

### Listening to real-time requests

On voice parts networks like Hotline HQ, you hear live broadcasts from yards and shops requesting specific parts. Spend a week listening and patterns emerge. If you keep hearing requests for 2018-2022 Hyundai Tucson transmissions and nobody on the line has one, that's a buy signal. Next time a damaged Tucson shows up at auction, you know the demand is there before you bid.

That's the difference between speculative buying and informed buying. You're not guessing which parts will sell. You're responding to demand you've already heard.

### Seasonal and regional patterns

Parts demand shifts with seasons and geography. AC compressors move in summer. Heater cores sell in winter. Body panels for popular trucks spike after hail season in the Midwest. If you track these patterns and time your auction purchases accordingly, you're buying into demand instead of hoping for it.

Combining this with real-time demand signals from voice parts networks and seasonal trends helps dismantlers buy vehicles that contain parts with verified, active buyer interest.
