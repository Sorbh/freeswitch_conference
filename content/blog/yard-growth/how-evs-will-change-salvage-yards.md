## When Will EVs Actually Show Up in Salvage Yards? {#ev-timeline}

Electric vehicles represent roughly 3% of US vehicles entering scrap channels today ([S&P Global Mobility](https://www.spglobal.com/mobility/), 2025). Industry projections put that figure at 8-12% by 2028 and 20-25% by 2032. The timeline gives you a preparation window, but it's narrower than most yard owners think.

Most EVs on the road right now are 2018 or newer. Teslas, Chevy Bolts, Nissan Leafs. They're still under warranty or with first owners who tend to keep them longer than average. When those vehicles age out, get totaled, or take flood damage, they'll flow into yards like any other wrecked car.

Geography matters. California, where EV adoption runs well ahead of the national curve, is already sending EVs to dismantlers at a higher rate. If you're running a yard in CA, TX, AZ, or FL, you'll see these vehicles before most. But every state catches up eventually.

The real inflection point isn't new EV sales. It's EV lease returns and insurance write-offs. Those events determine when EVs actually hit your lot. The first large wave of 3-year Tesla leases already expired in 2024-2025. Watch for a surge in totaled and auction-bound EVs from that cohort over the next 18 months.

> **Citation Capsule**: Electric vehicles currently make up approximately 3% of US vehicles entering scrap channels, with projections reaching 8-12% by 2028 and 20-25% by 2032, according to S&P Global Mobility (2025). This gives salvage yards a practical 3-5 year preparation window before EV volume becomes significant.

## The EV Recycling Market Is Already Massive {#ev-recycling-market}

The global EV battery recycling market is projected to reach $87.7 billion by 2032, growing at a 19.4% compound annual growth rate ([GlobeNewsWire](https://www.globenewswire.com/), 2026). Meanwhile, the broader vehicle recycling market hit $85.5 billion in 2025 and is forecast to reach $165.4 billion by 2033 ([DataBridge Market Research](https://www.databridgemarketresearch.com/), 2025).

Those numbers matter for one reason: the recycling industry is growing, not shrinking. Every year, Americans scrap roughly 12 to 15 million vehicles ([ISRI](https://www.isri.org/), 2025). That base volume isn't going away. EVs add another revenue layer on top of the ICE vehicles you already know how to process.

Here's the catch, though. EV recycling requires knowledge and equipment that most yards don't have yet. The yards that invest early will capture margins that latecomers can't. Sound familiar? It's the same pattern we saw with catalytic converter processing ten years ago.

## What Changes When You Dismantle an EV? {#what-changes}

EV battery packs contain 400 to 800 volts of stored energy, enough to kill on contact ([NHTSA](https://www.nhtsa.gov/), 2024). That single fact changes how you approach these vehicles. Here are the components that differ from ICE dismantling and what each one means for your daily workflow.

### High-voltage battery packs

This is the big one. Battery packs weigh 800 to 1,200 pounds, sit under the floor pan, and require specialized tools to remove safely. You can't just unbolt them the way you'd drop a transmission. Disconnect procedures vary by manufacturer. Tesla packs differ from Chevy packs differ from Ford packs. There's no universal method.

### Electric motors and drive units

EV motors look nothing like combustion engines. They're smaller, lighter, and contain rare-earth magnets and copper windings. Fewer moving parts mean fewer things to break. The challenge: most yards don't yet have the interchange data to identify which motors fit which vehicles.

### Inverters and power electronics

The inverter converts DC battery power to AC motor power. It's the brain of the drivetrain. These components are often manufacturer-specific and paired to the vehicle's VIN. Selling them used requires knowing which units are compatible across model years.

### Battery management systems

The BMS monitors every cell in the pack. It controls charging, discharging, and thermal management. A faulty BMS can make an otherwise healthy battery pack worthless, or dangerous. Testing capability matters here.

### Charging ports and onboard chargers

Different EVs use different charging standards: CCS, CHAdeMO, Tesla's NACS connector. Charging ports and onboard chargers are becoming a surprisingly in-demand used part as EV owners repair collision damage rather than total their vehicles.

I pulled my first hybrid battery pack in 2019. It took three hours because I had no idea what I was doing. The second one took forty-five minutes. By the fifth, it was routine. Full EV packs are the same learning curve, just steeper and with higher stakes if you skip the safety steps.

> **Citation Capsule**: EV battery packs operate at 400 to 800 volts per NHTSA (2024), requiring manufacturer-specific disconnect procedures, specialized removal equipment, and component knowledge that differs fundamentally from traditional ICE vehicle dismantling.

## What Stays the Same on an EV? {#what-stays-same}

Roughly 60-70% of an electric vehicle consists of conventional automotive parts that any experienced dismantler already knows how to handle ([Argonne National Laboratory](https://www.anl.gov/), 2024). Don't let the headlines fool you. The body, cabin, and chassis of an EV are still fundamentally a car.

Here's what doesn't change:

- **Body panels**: fenders, doors, hoods, trunk lids, bumper covers. Same materials, same removal process.
- **Glass**: windshields, side windows, rear glass. Identical to ICE vehicles in every practical way.
- **Suspension**: struts, control arms, knuckles, hubs, sway bars. Some EV components are beefier due to vehicle weight, but the parts are the same type.
- **Interior**: seats, dashboards, door panels, headliners, carpet. A Tesla seat comes out the same way a Honda seat does.
- **Wheels and tires**: standard bolt patterns, standard sizes. Nothing new here.
- **Lighting**: headlights, taillights, fog lights. LED assemblies on newer EVs carry solid resale value.
- **Brakes**: rotors, calipers, pads. EVs use regenerative braking, so brake parts often have less wear and sell at premium condition grades.

If you already know [which parts are most profitable to pull](/blog/yard-growth/most-profitable-parts-to-pull), many of those same categories apply to EVs. Body panels, glass, and lighting don't care what powers the drivetrain.


> **Citation Capsule**: Approximately 60-70% of an electric vehicle consists of conventional parts familiar to any auto dismantler, including body panels, glass, suspension, interior components, wheels, and brakes, according to Argonne National Laboratory (2024).

## EV Safety Gear and Training You Can't Skip {#ev-safety}

OSHA requires employers to provide documented high-voltage safety training before any worker handles EV battery systems ([OSHA](https://www.osha.gov/), 2025). This isn't optional, and it shouldn't be. A 400-volt shock can stop your heart in seconds. Here's the minimum gear and training your yard needs.

### Required PPE

- **Insulated gloves**: Class 0 or Class 00, rated for 1,000 volts or higher. These are the orange rubber gloves you'll see in every EV training photo. They must be tested every six months.
- **Face shield with arc rating**: protects against arc flash if a short circuit occurs during battery disconnect.
- **Arc-rated clothing**: FR-rated coveralls or a jacket and pants combo. Regular shop clothes won't protect you from an arc flash event.
- **Insulated tools**: standard wrenches and ratchets conduct electricity. You need tools specifically rated for high-voltage work.

### Battery disconnect procedures

Every manufacturer publishes a specific disconnect procedure. There's no universal method. Tesla, Ford, GM, Rivian, Hyundai: each one is different. The ARA and manufacturer service portals provide these documents. Print them. Laminate them. Hang them in the bay where the work happens.

### Fire suppression

Lithium-ion battery fires behave differently from gasoline fires. They can reignite hours after you think they're out. NFPA guidelines recommend 3,000 to 8,000 gallons of water for sustained direct cooling ([NFPA](https://www.nfpa.org/), 2024). You'll also need thermal monitoring equipment to confirm the pack has cooled completely before you move it.

### Storage requirements

Damaged EV batteries must be stored outdoors, away from structures, in containers rated for lithium-ion hazards. Check your local fire code and zoning. Many jurisdictions are still writing these rules, so stay current with your fire marshal's office.

> **Citation Capsule**: OSHA mandates documented high-voltage safety training for all EV battery handling, with NFPA recommending 3,000 to 8,000 gallons of water for lithium-ion fire suppression (NFPA, 2024). Required PPE includes Class 0 insulated gloves rated for 1,000 volts or higher, arc-rated face shields, and FR clothing.

## How Much Are Used EV Parts Actually Worth? {#ev-parts-value}

New EV battery packs cost $5,000 to $15,000 depending on the vehicle and pack size ([Recurrent Auto](https://www.recurrentauto.com/), 2025). New electric motors run $2,000 to $5,000 from the dealer. Those prices create a real opportunity for used parts sellers who can safely extract, test, and price these components.

### Battery modules and cells

Full battery packs carry the highest value, but individual modules and cells sell well too. A single module from a Tesla Model 3 Long Range pack can fetch $800 to $2,000 depending on state of health. Buyers include DIY EV builders, solar storage installers, and other EV owners looking to extend their vehicle's range.

### Electric motors

A used Tesla drive unit in good condition sells for $1,500 to $3,500. That's a solid markup over your acquisition cost, especially if you pulled the vehicle from auction at a reasonable price. The motor is often the most profitable single component after the battery pack itself.

### Inverters, chargers, and electronics

Lower volume but higher margin. Onboard chargers sell for $300 to $800 used. Inverters range from $500 to $1,500. The market for these parts is still developing, which means less price competition for yards that get in early.

Across the yards I've talked to over the past year, EV parts consistently command a 30-50% premium over comparable ICE parts in the same vehicle class. A used Tesla headlight assembly, for example, sells for significantly more than a used Camry headlight, even though the removal process is identical. The premium comes from scarcity and high new-part pricing.

> **Citation Capsule**: New EV battery packs cost $5,000 to $15,000 and electric motors cost $2,000 to $5,000 from the dealer, according to Recurrent Auto (2025), creating substantial resale margins for salvage yards that can safely extract, test, and certify these high-value components.

## How Should Your Yard Prepare for EVs Right Now? {#prepare-now}

Roughly 9,000 auto recycling businesses operate across the US ([ARA](https://a-r-a.org/), 2025), and the vast majority have zero EV dismantling experience. That's not a criticism. It's an opportunity. Starting now puts you ahead of nearly everyone, and you don't need to overhaul your entire operation to begin.

### Year one: training and small investments

Start with ARA's EV safety and dismantling courses. They're affordable and industry-recognized. Get at least two team members certified. Buy your first set of insulated tools and Class 0 gloves. Total investment: roughly $2,000 to $5,000.

Begin dismantling hybrid vehicles if you haven't already. Hybrids are the on-ramp. Their battery packs run at lower voltage, weigh less, and are simpler to handle. You'll learn the disconnect procedures and PPE habits at lower risk before full BEVs show up in volume.

### Year two: equipment and storage

Invest in a battery lift table, insulated storage containers, and a dedicated EV bay with proper ventilation. Budget $10,000 to $25,000 depending on your yard's size and local code requirements.

Update your [yard management software](/blog/yard-growth/salvage-yard-software-compared) to track EV-specific parts with proper interchange data. Hollander is expanding its EV coverage, and your inventory system needs to keep pace with the parts you're pulling.


### Year three and beyond: specialization

As EV volume grows, consider designating team members as EV specialists. Build relationships with EV repair shops, fleet operators, and battery recycling companies. These are your future buyers, and they're already looking for reliable salvage sources.

One practical step: when unfamiliar EV components come through your yard, voice networks connecting you to other dismantlers can help you identify, price, and source parts you've never seen before. That kind of peer knowledge matters when you're handling a component for the first time.

Does all this sound like a lot? It is. But so was learning to deal with airbag systems in the 1990s, and catalytic converter regulations in the 2000s. The yards that adapted early to those changes made money. The rest played catch-up for years.

> **Citation Capsule**: The ARA reports roughly 9,000 US auto recycling businesses (2025), with the vast majority lacking EV dismantling experience. Practical preparation starts at $2,000 to $5,000 for training and PPE in year one, scaling to $10,000 to $25,000 for equipment and dedicated EV bays by year two.

## FAQ {#faq}

### Do salvage yards need a special license to dismantle EVs?

Requirements vary by state. California requires EV-specific dismantler endorsements through the DMV. Most states are still developing their regulations. Contact your state's DMV and the [Automotive Recyclers Association](https://a-r-a.org/) for current requirements. At minimum, OSHA mandates high-voltage safety training for any worker handling EV batteries ([OSHA](https://www.osha.gov/), 2025).

### Can you put water on a lithium battery fire?

Yes, but you need a lot of it. NFPA guidelines recommend 3,000 to 8,000 gallons of water for sustained direct cooling of a lithium-ion battery fire ([NFPA](https://www.nfpa.org/), 2024). Standard extinguishers won't cut it. Many fire departments now carry thermal imaging cameras to monitor battery cell temperatures for hours after the visible flames are gone. Plan your storage accordingly.

### Are hybrid vehicles a good starting point for EV dismantling?

Hybrids are the best entry point, hands down. Their battery packs are smaller, lower voltage, and easier to handle than full BEV packs. You learn disconnect procedures, PPE habits, and storage protocols at lower risk. Most yards already have hybrids sitting on their lots right now. Start there before committing budget to full EV training and equipment.
