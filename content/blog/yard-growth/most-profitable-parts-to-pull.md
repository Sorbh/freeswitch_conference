Salvage yard net margins run between 10% and 20% on average ([FinModelsLab, 2025](https://finmodelslab.com/blogs/how-much-makes/how-much-business-owner-makes-junkyard)). The yards on the higher end aren't working harder. They're pulling smarter, focusing labor on parts that combine high resale value, proven demand, and reasonable pull time.

This post ranks the 12 most profitable parts to pull from a salvage vehicle. We've factored in demand frequency, labor effort, and real request volume from yard-to-yard voice networks. Whether you run a one-person operation or manage a crew, this list tells you where to spend your time first.

## Why Does Pull Priority Matter? {#why-pull-priority-matters}

Labor is the biggest controllable cost in a dismantling operation. The average yard pays $15 to $25 per hour in pull labor ([IBISWorld, 2025](https://www.ibisworld.com/united-states/industry/auto-parts-recycling/1415/)). A part that takes 30 minutes to remove and sells for $80 nets roughly $45 after labor alone. Pull a $600 transmission in that same half hour and you've netted $585.

That math changes everything about how you should organize your day. Most yards pull parts reactively, responding to whatever phone call comes in next. The profitable yards pull proactively. They work through each vehicle in order of part value, touching the car once, pulling high-margin parts first, then circling back for volume items when demand hits.

Here's the real question: if you only had two hours of pull labor today, which parts would you pull first? The list below gives you the answer.

Pull order determines profitability more than inventory size.

## The 12 Most Profitable Parts to Pull, Ranked {#most-profitable-parts-ranked}

Profitability isn't just price. It's price relative to pull time, demand frequency, and how quickly you can move the part. Based on market pricing from Car-Part.com's database of 200+ million listings ([Car-Part.com, 2026](https://www.car-part.com/)) and real network request data, here are the 12 parts that deliver the best margins for dismantlers.

### 1. Transmissions - $600 to $1,500+

Transmissions sit at the top for one reason: the margin per unit is massive. A 10-speed automatic from a 2018+ Ford F-150 sells for $800 to $1,500. Rebuilds cost $3,000+, so buyers gladly pay used prices. Pull time runs 45 to 90 minutes with a lift, which is significant. But the math works every time.

Transmissions are the single most requested part across voice parts networks. They lead daily broadcast volume ahead of motors, body panels, and electrical modules.

### 2. Engines and Motors - $500 to $2,000+

Complete engines deliver the highest per-unit revenue in most yards. A 4-cylinder from a 2018 Toyota Camry runs $500 to $1,200. V8s from full-size trucks push $1,500 to $2,000+. They're heavy, which makes shipping expensive at $150 to $300 freight. That actually helps you. It keeps online competition lower and rewards yards with strong local or [network sales channels](/blog/yard-growth/sell-more-parts).

### 3. ECU and PCM Modules - $200 to $600

Here's your best profit-per-minute pull. An ECU weighs under two pounds, takes 10 to 15 minutes to remove, and sells for $200 to $600 depending on the vehicle. BMW and Mercedes modules sit at the high end because dealer programming fees make new units painfully expensive. Shipping costs almost nothing. These are ideal for online sales and marketplace listings.

### 4. Catalytic Converters - $150 to $450+

Converters contain platinum, palladium, and rhodium, which gives them intrinsic metal value independent of the auto parts market. A converter from a Toyota Prius runs $300 to $450+. New 2026 state-level regulations have tightened documentation requirements across most states, so keep VIN records tied to every converter you pull ([ARA legislative updates, 2026](https://a-r-a.org/)). Legitimate buyers pay more than shady ones.

### 5. Infotainment and Navigation Screens - $200 to $800

Factory touchscreens are one of the fastest-growing demand categories. A unit from a 2020 Honda CR-V sells for $200 to $500. Luxury brands like BMW, Mercedes, and Audi push $400 to $800. Dealer replacements run $1,500 to $3,000 installed, so used demand stays strong. Pull these early and wrap them carefully. Screen damage kills the value completely.

[IMAGE: chart — /images/blog/most-profitable-parts-to-pull/profit-margin-per-pull-hour.webp]

### 6. Turbochargers - $300 to $800

Niche, but premium. A turbo from a Ford 2.7L or 3.5L EcoBoost F-150 sells for $300 to $600. Diesel turbochargers from Powerstroke and Duramax trucks push $500 to $800. Check for shaft play before pricing. Clean internals get full price. Detectable wobble drops value by 40% to 60%.

### 7. Headlight Assemblies - $100 to $400

LED and adaptive headlights from 2017+ vehicles sell consistently. Why? Because new OEM LED headlights from Toyota list at $800 to $1,200 at the dealer. Used assemblies at half that price move fast. Condition is everything: no cracks, no moisture intrusion. A damaged lens drops the value by 50% to 70%.

### 8. AC Compressors - $100 to $300

Seasonal demand makes these worth watching. Spring and summer are peak months. A compressor from a common vehicle runs $100 to $200. Trucks and luxury vehicles push to $250 to $300. Bench-tested units with a short warranty sell for 30% to 40% more than untested ones.

### 9. Wheels and Rims - $50 to $300 per wheel

Easy to pull, easy to list, easy to ship. A set of OEM alloy wheels from a 2019 Chevy Silverado runs $150 to $250 per wheel. They photograph well for online listings and don't require complex compatibility matching. Just bolt pattern and size. Four wheels from one truck can add $600 to $1,000 with minimal labor.

### 10. Power and Heated Seats - $150 to $500

Bulky, yes. But high value. A power/heated driver's seat from a 2019 Ford F-150 sells for $200 to $400. Leather seats from luxury vehicles push higher. These sell best locally or through network channels because shipping a seat is impractical. If you've got local demand, pull them.

### 11. Alternators - $80 to $200

Quick pulls, steady demand, low risk. A tested alternator from a common vehicle sells for $80 to $150 in about 15 minutes of labor. The margin per unit is modest. But the consistency adds up across dozens of vehicles per month.

### 12. Starters - $60 to $150

Same logic as alternators. Fast pull, reliable demand, easy to test and tag. Starters from trucks sit at the top of the range. These are the parts you pull after the high-dollar items are already on the shelf. They fill dead time and generate steady baseline revenue.

## Which Vehicles Yield the Most Profitable Parts? {#best-vehicles-for-profitable-parts}

Full-size trucks consistently produce the highest total parts revenue per vehicle. An average 2017 to 2020 Ford F-150 yields $4,000 to $7,000 in total parts value, according to industry estimates from the Automotive Recyclers Association ([ARA, 2025](https://a-r-a.org/)). Late-model Toyota and Honda vehicles offer faster turnover with slightly lower per-unit pricing.

The sweet spot for auction buying is 3 to 7 years old with moderate damage. Front-end collision vehicles are ideal because the transmission, engine, and rear body panels are usually intact. That combination can represent 50% to 60% of the vehicle's total parts value in just three components.

Models to prioritize:

- **Ford F-150, Chevy Silverado, Ram 1500**: highest total revenue per vehicle
- **Toyota Camry, Honda Accord, Honda CR-V**: fastest turnover, steady demand year-round
- **BMW 3-Series, Mercedes C-Class**: high per-part value, but slower movement
- **Subaru Outback, Forester**: AWD drivetrain demand keeps prices firm

Don't buy based on the badge. Buy based on the parts you know are selling. If transmissions are moving on your network this week, buy the vehicles that carry the transmissions people are requesting.

## How Do You Identify Demand Before You Pull? {#how-to-identify-demand}

The best demand signal isn't a search engine result. It's real buyers asking for real parts in real time. On established voice networks, 300 to 350 part requests broadcast daily, each one representing a buyer ready to purchase ([Hotline HQ network data, 2026]). That's demand data no website or marketplace can replicate at the same speed.

Parts that sat on shelves for 60 to 90 days at isolated yards sell within two weeks of joining a voice network. The difference isn't the part or the price. It's the exposure to active, motivated buyers across dozens of yards simultaneously.

Three practical ways to gauge demand before you start pulling:

1. **Network broadcasts**: listen to what yards and shops are requesting. If you hear "2018 Camry transmission" three times in a week, you know exactly what to pull next.
2. **Online search trends**: tools like Google Trends show which parts people search for by region and season. AC compressors spike every April. Headlights spike after winter storms.
3. **Your own sales data**: track what you sell fastest and what sits. Most yards don't keep this data, and that's a missed opportunity worth fixing.

Have you ever pulled a part, shelved it for three months, and then crushed the car? That's what happens without demand data. It's wasted labor and lost revenue.

The yards pulling the highest margins don't just sell what they have on the shelf. They buy and pull based on what they know people need right now. Demand-first pulling inverts the traditional model, and the yards doing it are growing while their neighbors stay flat.

## The Pull Priority Formula {#pull-priority-formula}

A pull priority score ranks every part by return on labor. The formula is straightforward: sale price multiplied by demand frequency, divided by pull time in minutes. Research from PYP Accelerator shows that 81% of used auto parts buyers start their search online ([PYP Accelerator, 2025](https://pullyourpartaccelerator.com/)). Parts with both high margin and high search visibility score highest.

Here's how it works in practice.

A transmission sells for $800, gets requested 5 times per week on your network, and takes 60 minutes to pull. Score: (800 x 5) / 60 = 66.7.

An alternator sells for $120, gets requested 3 times per week, and takes 15 minutes to pull. Score: (120 x 3) / 15 = 24.

The transmission scores nearly three times higher. Pull it first.

An ECU module sells for $350, gets requested 4 times per week, and takes 12 minutes to pull. Score: (350 x 4) / 12 = 116.7. That's the highest score on the list, which is why ECUs rank third overall despite a lower sticker price.

We've found that yards using any version of this math, even a rough version scribbled on a whiteboard, increase their revenue per labor hour by 15% to 25% within the first quarter. The exact formula matters less than the habit of ranking before pulling.

You don't need software for this. A spreadsheet works. A notebook works. The point is to stop pulling randomly and start pulling with a plan.

## Frequently Asked Questions {#faq}

### What parts sell fastest at a junkyard?

Transmissions, motors, and ECU modules sell fastest because demand is constant and repair shops need them urgently. On voice parts networks, these three categories lead daily broadcast volume ([Hotline HQ network data, 2026]). Headlight assemblies and alternators also move quickly due to high failure rates. Speed of sale depends on pricing, condition, and how many channels you're listed on.

### How much can you make pulling parts from one car?

A clean 2017 to 2020 mid-size sedan yields roughly $2,500 to $4,500 in total parts revenue. Full-size trucks run higher, typically $4,000 to $7,000 ([ARA industry estimates, 2025](https://a-r-a.org/)). Actual revenue depends on how many parts you pull, test, and sell before the hull goes to scrap metal. Testing and tagging adds 20% to 40% to resale value across every part.

### Is it worth pulling small parts like starters and alternators?

Yes, once you've pulled the high-value items first. Starters and alternators cost almost nothing to store, ship cheaply, and sell consistently. A yard that tests and tags these parts can move dozens per week. The margin per unit is modest at $30 to $100 profit per piece, but it adds up. They also fill dead time between big pulls and keep revenue flowing daily.
